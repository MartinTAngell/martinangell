I’ve finally finished the code for my personal website, and what’s the first thing I want to do? Complain about how so many people screw up the role of Éponine in Les Misérables (Les Mis). This is something of a running joke in my family and among certain corners of my circle of friends. You see, I’ve been complaining about this since at least junior year of high school, around 2021-2022. So, naturally, I’ve given this rant a great many times in person, but I felt this was the perfect occasion to codify my argument for how I’d like to see Éponine performed.

Of course, I am obliged to put a massive **SPOILER WARNING** here. This will spoil major and rather emotional events in both the musical Les Mis and the book it is based upon. Spoilers from a 164-year-old book don’t really count, do they?

First, a bit of context. I will assume that whoever is reading this does not know anything about Les Mis. If you are an expert on this subject, like myself, feel free to skip to after the next video. Les Mis follows Jean Valjean, an ex-convict who breaks his parole and changes his identity after being denied every possible opportunity because of his criminal record. After his actions, very indirectly, lead to the death of Fantine, a factory worker in Valjean’s employ who was terminated for rather bigoted reasons, he decides he must take responsibility for her daughter, Cosette. All the while, he is pursued by a zealous police officer named Javert (you may have seen comical and frankly embarrassing videos of [Russell Crowe singing the line “and I’m Javert!”](https://youtu.be/I8WSysB5vKM?si=3cBXa8JvZzO-q9sB&t=49) from the absolutely abysmal movie adaptation, but that’s a topic for another day).

Anyway, Cosette falls in love with Marius, a rich boy estranged from his family over politics who is now part of a student rebellion. However, there is already someone in love with Marius: Éponine. Marius is either oblivious to Éponine’s affections or (more likely) callously stringing her along. When the students rebel against the French government, Éponine is shot and killed while trying to crawl over the barricades (pictured below) to get to Marius after delivering a love letter he wrote to Cosette.

![The barricades featured in Act 2 of Les Misérables](/rants/barricades.jpeg)

The story of Les Mis is significantly longer than this summary. It would take its own page on this website to fully explain, so I’ll leave you with that summary of events related to Éponine in the musical before we get into the added context of the book. If you’re just now making your acquaintance with this mammoth of a musical, which happens to be my favorite ever, I cannot recommend enough that you watch the 1987 Tony Award performance from the original Broadway production (attached below).

![The original Broadway production of Les Misérables performing at the 1987 Tony Awards](https://youtu.be/x-lB0PL9TJs?si=hppuwDCQb_HT6nn_)

In my opinion, Éponine is the ultimate tragic figure in Les Mis, especially in the book. I will provide some details we get in the book that aren’t in the musical, as they are incredibly relevant to my overarching argument. When Éponine is young, she is pampered and comfortable. Eventually, her father, an antagonist of both the book and musical, goes bankrupt and loses his inn and source of income. Now, Éponine is thrust into poverty and must become resourceful or die. Her only true happiness comes from Marius. When Marius falls in love with Cosette, Éponine realizes he will never love her, so she decides that if she can’t be his, they will die together. Éponine then encourages Marius to go and fight (and likely die) at the barricades. When she arrives at the barricade, Marius is about to be shot, so she puts her hand in front of the musket and is fatally shot to save Marius’ life. In the end, her love for Marius is stronger than her jealousy towards Cosette. Before she dies, she asks Marius to kiss her forehead upon her death, saying, “I shall feel it”; he does.

Éponine expresses many traits that could indicate clinical depression. She suffers from tremendously low self-worth, chronic hopelessness, emotional dependence, and suicidal ideation. The question of whether Marius’ rejection or her dire socio-economic status is the main cause of her despair is very open, but it would be best to assume a combination of the two.

With this reading of Éponine in mind, I will finally give my argument for how I would like to see Éponine portrayed in the stage musical. Éponine’s big solo number in Les Mis (and the second-best song of the musical behind “One Day More”) is called “On My Own.” It takes place early in Act 2, right after she has delivered Marius’ love letter. It follows Éponine walking through the streets of Paris, singing to herself about how she likes to imagine Marius is with her. It’s a beautiful private confession that perfectly exemplifies Éponine’s despair and how deep her desire for Marius goes.

> Sometimes, I walk alone at night\
> When everybody else is sleeping\
> I think of him, and then I'm happy\
> With the company I'm keeping.\
> The city goes to bed,\
> And I can live inside my head.

In both the original London and Broadway productions, Éponine was played by Frances Ruffelle. This performance won her the Tony Award for Best Featured Actress in a Musical in 1987, and I will explain why I believe that she is the best ever to perform this role and why I think some of the other highly regarded actresses who have tackled Éponine have fallen short. First, I have here a video of Ruffelle performing the number in 1997 (the videos of her performing it from earlier are all flawed in their recording quality, as is this one, but less so). 

![Frances Ruffelle performing "On My Own" in 1997](https://youtu.be/hktaR4SZiVE?si=frxZs7K2bkQnMSVl)

So, what is it that I think she did so well that others didn’t? Her performance of “On My Own” is intimate in the most beautiful way. Then, she gets to the bridge, and my heart breaks.

> And I know\
> It's only in my mind,\
> That I'm talking to myself\
> And not to him.\
> And although I know that he is blind,\
> Still, I say,\
> There's a way for us

(2:12 to 2:38)

It sounds like she’s holding back tears for the entire passage. This is a moment where Éponine is in a strange state between sadness and hope. She knows that Marius will never love her, but she holds on to some hope: “Still, I say, there’s a way for us.” I see no argument for Éponine to be depicted as angry in this moment, and if she is, who is she angry with? Marius? She could never be angry with Marius. Herself? I don’t think so. I read this phrase more as one of self-pity; she’s lamenting her inability to live with the truth that Marius doesn’t love her. Why then do so many incredible actresses decide that they want to really heighten the dynamics of this section and get loud? 

For example, Lea Salonga, one of my favorite actresses and among the greatest Éponines of all time, gets this wrong in the 10th Anniversary Concert (1995; see below, 2:44 to 3:11)

![Lea Salonga performing "On My Own" in 1995](https://youtu.be/VjfmP7h3gBw?si=APgdsp8xQAABJbE5)

On the phrase “that I’m talking to myself,” Salonga’s Éponine sounds resentful; she sounds disgusted by her own proclivity for imagining life with Marius. Those are Éponine’s only reprieves from her sorrows: her time spent with Marius and her time spent imagining life with Marius. Why does she sound angry? I believe it comes down to a misreading of the character in this moment. The Éponine that we see Salonga portray in this moment isn’t mournful of the life that could never be; she’s angry. Similarly, Samantha Barks gets loud on the line “I know that he is blind” in the 25th Anniversary Concert (2010). She did a better job in the movie, but that doesn’t count; I hate that movie.

There is only one passage where Ruffelle truly gets loud.

> I love him,\
> But every day I'm learning\
> All my life,\
> I've only been pretending!\
> Without me,\
> His world will go on turning,\
> A world that's full of happiness that I have never known!

(3:01 to 3:28, there’s a glitch in the recording towards the end: please don’t hold that against Frances Ruffelle.)

So, how is that different from the previous lines? Why is it, in my opinion, okay to get loud here and not before? The bridge is Éponine still in her state of imagination; she insists “there’s a way for us.” However, in this final section, she finally says the truth: “Without me, his world will go on turning.” That is the moment we see Éponine truly realize that Marius will never love her and that if she were gone, he would be fine. It mirrors an earlier line: “Without him, I feel his arms around me.” When Éponine doesn’t have Marius, she imagines he’s there. Éponine realizes that when she is gone, Marius will live the same life he has, which unfortunately ends up being rather true. She could survive without him by imagining Marius; Marius could survive without her by just continuing to live. The other key line is “A world that’s full of happiness that I have never known!” Ruffelle’s Éponine shows some real anger in this moment, but it’s not at Marius or herself. It’s at the world. She’s angry at the fact that she has never gotten to experience happiness. Even in her anger, Ruffelle’s Éponine sounds like she’s on the verge of tears. Salonga and Barks get this right as well, so credit where credit is due.

My preference for Ruffelle’s performance isn’t just personal taste. There’s strong evidence that her approach is more in line with the original vision of the show’s creators, composer Claude-Michel Schönberg and lyricist Alain Boublil. In an [interview with Broadway World](https://www.broadwayworld.com/article/InDepth-InterView-Frances-Ruffelle-Talks-54-Below-Shows-LES-MISERABLES-Broadway-Hollywood-Upcoming-Projects-More-20130914), Ruffelle spoke about her experience auditioning for the part.

> He [Trevor Nunn, co-director of the original production] asked if I would sing a song for the French composers - this Édith Piaf song. So, they thought of the character of Éponine as this street singer type character and they sort of based her on Édith Piaf, so they wanted to hear me do that kind of thing.

The song she was asked to sing was “Hymne à l'amour.” I have attached Piaf’s version below if you are interested. 

![Édith Piaf's "Hymne à l'amour."](https://youtu.be/sgBldNsK-xw?si=jtpoTuy0ZPsyyFIf)

The role was modeled, at least in part, after the voice of Édith Piaf, which is clear when you listen to Ruffelle’s performance of the song. It’s not that Piaf never gets loud or sounds like she’s always crying, but there’s a rawness to her voice that you can hear in Ruffelle’s too.

Éponine was originally intended to be, vocally, more along the lines of a street singer than a modern Broadway diva, belting as loud as they can. She was meant to be a character who really feels her emotions and lets them take hold of her. Éponine is as emotionally vivid as any character for the stage, so it breaks my heart to see her portrayed in a way that I think really strips the depth from her. The next time I see Les Mis, I’d love to see the actress playing Éponine really live in that sorrow for the duration of the song. I hope she feels the grief of Marius not loving her without turning it into anger, and that she feels the anger that Éponine has with the world, not with herself or Marius. I hope she makes me cry, because no Éponine I have seen live has accomplished that, although it is an admittedly very difficult thing to do. 

One last thing I should make clear. I love Lea Salonga, and I love Samantha Barks, so please don’t come away from this thinking that I don’t. Their portrayals of Éponine are, mostly, fantastic and really sell the character. However, this song is so crucial to her character that it must be done correctly. I truly think you lose so much by not delving into the book's interpretation of Éponine, which I feel Ruffelle really captured. Salonga and Barks’ performances have become the standard that I see most imitated in productions nowadays, which makes sense considering so many performers want that grand epic number that brings the audience to their feet. I would love to see a performer do what Ruffelle did and put the character first. I really hope I see that one day.
