Those who know me know that I will dispense random historical facts at seemingly arbitrary moments. For someone whose background is in computing, I know a weird amount about history, but I think I fixate on a rather atypical part of history. Many people are fascinated by World War 2 or the Civil War, and there’s a common joke that men are always fixating on the Roman Empire. I, however, have a keen interest in the history of the Papacy. Still, if you ask me how often I think about the Roman Empire, it’s most days. Because I want to highlight a diversity of interests on this page, even though I could write 20 posts about Les Misérables, I wanted to tell you about a fascinating period in Papal history: The Western Schism.

I think many of us are well aware of the Great Schism, which in 1054 divided the Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church, a division that lasts to this day. The associated excommunications have been lifted since 1965. However, the schism that I’m interested in discussing occurred from 1378 to 1417. Strangely enough, people often refer to the Western Schism as the Great Schism, which I’ve found blatantly confusing.

We begin our story, however, even earlier, in 1304 with the death of Pope Benedict XI. At this point, the college of cardinals was a rather even split between French and Italian cardinals. Interestingly enough, the conclave assembled in Perugia, where Benedict XI had died. The disputes between the French and Italian cardinals resulted in the conclave taking just under a year to elect Pope Clement V, a non-cardinal and the archbishop of Bordeaux. It is important to note that while he was a subject of England, he was a childhood friend of King Philip IV of France. 
![Pope Benedict XI](/rants/three-popes/benedictxi.jpg)

While not exactly a puppet, Clement V is believed to have given certain political concessions to Philip IV in return for his assistance in getting elected to the Papacy. Philip IV’s influence over Clement V was significant and resulted in the suppression and trial of the Knights Templar in France. The trial of the Knights Templar was to take place in Vienne (the one in France, not Vienna in Austria), so Clement V, in 1309, traveled to nearby Avignon, in southeastern France, although it was part of the Holy Roman Empire at this time. The cited reason for moving the curia was instability and unsafe conditions in Rome. He had intended to return to Rome at some point following the trial. However, Clement V’s health declined heavily during and after the trial. He died at Roquemaure near Avignon in 1314.

Strangely enough, the curia didn’t move back to Rome. It stayed in Avignon until 1376. The popes who ruled from Avignon were still the bishops of Rome. Pope Gregory XI was the seventh pope to rule from Avignon, and also the most recent French-born pope. Papal authority in Italy had been slipping to the point of religious conflict, and Saint Catherine of Siena was begging Pope Gregory XI to return to Italy. Eventually, he relented and returned to Rome, and subsequently died. Lots of dead popes in this rant. Not-so-fun fact: the average Papacy in history lasted roughly seven and a half years.
![Fresco of Pope Gregory XI Returning to Rome](/rants/three-popes/returntorome.jpg)

After Gregory XI died in Rome, a mob of Italians accosted the conclave, even raiding their wine cellar. They desperately wanted an Italian pope and a permanent return to the curia residing in Rome. So, the conclave chose Bartolomeo Prignano, an Italian by birth, to be the new pope: Urban VI. Fun fact: Pope Urban VI was the last non-cardinal to be chosen as pope. Immediately after his election, Urban VI began instructing the cardinals that they were not to accept any gifts, monetary or otherwise, from anyone outside the church to avoid undue influence. This was probably a response, in part, to the heavy bribery involved in French influence over the papacy. Additionally, Urban VI was rather abusive to the cardinals, having tortured some and disappeared others. The French cardinals did not take too kindly to this and held a council near Rome where they decreed that Pope Urban VI was not truly pope, as he was elected under undue influence from the Italian mob. So, they (under heavy influence from King Charles V of France) chose a new pope, who took the name Clement VII and resided in Avignon.

So, now we’ve got two popes. Clement VII was recognized by France, Castile, Aragon, Navarre, and Scotland, while Urban VI was recognized by England, the Holy Roman Empire, and most of Italy. In order to end this schism, a church council would have to be called, but neither pope would call a council for fear of being stripped of their position and the other being fully recognized as pope. In 1409, as talks between both sides broke down, the associated cardinals of each pope turned coat and all met together at the Council of Pisa. They decided to depose both the Avignonian pope, Benedict XIII, and the Roman pope, Gregory XII. They raised up the archbishop of Milan to be the new pope to unify all sides. To nobody’s surprise, neither Benedict XIII nor Gregory XII recognized him as pope or abdicated.

So, now we’ve got three popes. In 1414, the Council of Constance, endorsed by the Roman and Pisan popes, was held. It decreed that the council was the church’s most powerful governing body and had the supreme power to depose popes. The council decided that all three popes should abdicate and a new one would be selected. The Roman pope, Gregory XII, agreed and stepped down. However, the Pisan pope John XXIII, who had called the council (under significant pressure from the Emperor), fled dressed as a postman. In absentia, he was deposed by the council, and was later incarcerated. In Avignon, Benedict XIII refused to step down, but he had effectively no power at this point. The cardinals at the council chose Oddone Colonna, who took the name Martin V (I like that name for no particular reason).
![Timeline of the papacy during the western schism](/rants/three-popes/timeline.png)

So, now we’ve got two popes again, but one of them was powerless, and the other was rather universally recognized. The Avignon antipapacy at this point was effectively a joke. Benedict XIII stayed in a seaside castle in Spain until his death, maintaining that he was the true pope the entire time. His successor, Clement VIII, resigned in 1429 and recognized Martin V.

So, now we’ve got one pope, Martin V, who ruled from Rome. He was also the last pope to take maybe the best first name ever, so if any future popes are reading this, I have ideas. The Catholic Church views the Roman line of the papacy as being the rightful one. So, the official list of popes follows the original Avignon popes and then Urban VI, Boniface IX, Innocent VII, Gregory XII, and then Martin V. The popes who ruled in Avignon and Pisa after Gregory XI are now considered antipopes. Another fun fact is that the Catholic Church has said that the decree that the Council of Constance held power over even the pope was invalid, as it was issued before the council was supported by a recognized pope.

So, why should you care? I don’t know that you should; I just felt it was interesting. In all honesty, I just have a lot of random historical stories that I find fascinating, and I enjoy sharing them. My brother, Bill (wonderful fellow), believes that history is best told and taught as a story. A string of events connected by an underlying thread. I like to think that this is a story of the influence and response to monarchical influence over the papacy. This is not the entire story; there is a lot more to it, and maybe one day soon I’ll write about the influence of the Holy Roman Empire on the papacy.
