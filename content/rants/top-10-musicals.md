# #10: Natasha, Pierre & The Great Comet of 1812 (2016)
**Music, Lyrics, Book, and Orchestrations by Dave Malloy.**
![Natasha Pierre & The Great Comet of 1812](/rants/top10musicals/greatcomet.jpeg)

Those who know me will not be surprised that this musical, which has largely faded into obscurity in the minds of Broadway audiences, made my list. This show adapts approximately 70 pages of Leo Tolstoy’s masterpiece, War and Peace, which happens to be my favorite book. However, I did mention that this list was unbiased, so that’s irrelevant to it making the list. Great Comet is a sung-through musical, so its strengths and weaknesses are found in the score. Great Comet has one of the best opening numbers I’ve ever heard; “Prologue” introduces all the characters, sets the tone perfectly, and primes you for a beautiful story of love, war, and Russia.

Additionally, numbers like “Pierre,” “No One Else,” “Dust and Ashes,” and “The Abduction” are phenomenal and create such memorable characters and plot moments. However, the second act really slows, and the show loses a lot of steam after intermission and towards the end of the show. I cannot recommend this show enough to anyone who loves Tolstoy as much as I do or who is looking for something new to listen to. It could have ranked higher if it was a more consistently great show, but in its current form, it’s composed of great moments tied together with thin cartilage.

# #9: A Gentleman’s Guide to Love and Murder (2013)
**Music by Steven Lutvak, Lyrics by Robert L. Freedman and Steven Lutvak, Book by Robert L. Freedman, Orchestrated by Jonathan Tunick.**
![A Gentleman's Guide to Love and Murder](/rants/top10musicals/gentsguide.jpg)

Another relatively short-lived 2010s musical… Really, Marty? Yes, get off my back.  You can’t call it a bad pick when it won the Tony Award for Best Musical and 3 other Tony Awards. A spoiler for this list: this is the only pure comedy. And it’s a really funny show. Gent’s Guide is the story of Montague "Monty" Navarro learning that he isn’t of the poor birth he thought he was, but actually he’s 9th in line to the vast D’Ysquith fortune, including the Earldom of Highhurst, and methodically killing all of his relatives ahead of him in line. The main gimmick, which induces many of the laughs, is that all the other D’Ysquiths (except for Monty’s eventual wife, Phoebe) are played by the same actor. In the original production, it was the incredible Jefferson Mays.

The manner in which Monty kills his relatives gets more and more ridiculous as the show goes on. He’ll try anything from overloading a barbell, which will get heavy and fall, decapitating a eugenicist bodybuilder, to sending a relative to the Punjab on a charitable mission during a malaria epidemic. The show is downright hilarious, and the score really shines with some great comedic numbers, including the funeral crowd number “Why Are All the D’Ysquiths Dying,” and the ridiculously funny “I’ve Decided to Marry You.”

While I love this show very much, I think the fact that it is so focused on being purely a comedy prevents it from ranking higher on this list. I think we’ll find that as we get closer to the top, the themes get more and more serious, as do the shows.

# #8: Into the Woods (1987)
**Music and Lyrics by Stephen Sondheim, Book by James Lapine, Orchestrated by Jonathan Tunick.**
![Into the Woods](/rants/top10musicals/intothewoods.jpg)

“But, Marty,” I hear you say, “Into the Woods is a comedy! You said Gent’s Guide was the only comedy on this list!” To that I say, you could not be more wrong. Into the Woods is not a comedy. It’s funny, but it’s not a comedy. That could be its own rant, so I won’t even cover that take. 

I will also point out the significance of this being a Sondheim show, which automatically makes me like it more. You’ll be seeing that name again a few times before this list is over.

Into the Woods puts Cinderella, Little Red Ridinghood, Jack and the Beanstalk, and Rapunzel into the same world, with all of their stories happening at once. However, there is an entirely original story featuring a childless Baker and his wife who discover that they are subject to a witch’s curse. At the end of Act 1, all the characters have their greatest wishes fulfilled, but Act 2 shows that this did not prepare them for the real world. 

Into the Woods is fascinating in that Act 1 could be its own one-act musical with a happy ending, and I’d argue that it wouldn’t be particularly deep. All of the meaning in the show really comes from Act 2, when the characters are forced to make consequential decisions without guidance or magic. The important thing that the show is trying to tell us is that if you’re always given exactly what you want and you never have to work for it, you’ll never develop the skills you need to get the things you want in the future. 

To any Into the Woods fans out there, I would recommend [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NUN_MTChn5M) of Sondheim and Lapine discussing how to do a good production of Into the Woods, which really goes deep into the musical and literary construction of the show. I would also recommend [this video by Sideways](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9b6OEjF4s1M), who consistently provides some of the best musical theater analysis on the internet.

# #7: Pippin (1972)*
**Music and Lyrics by Stephen Schwartz, Book by Roger O. Hirson and Bob Fosse, Orchestrated by Ralph Burns.**
![Pippin](/rants/top10musicals/pippin.webp)

Pippin is another show mistakenly called a comedy. I know I’m very strict with the term. I’ll cover it some other time. It’s a dark drama that’s very funny. Pippin tells the fictionalized life story of the firstborn son of Charlemagne, Pepin the Hunchback. The fictional Pippin is a young man, crown prince of the Holy Roman Empire, and unsure of his place in the world, but determined to live an extraordinary life. (I find it important to note that the real Pippin was never a crown prince and the Holy Roman Empire didn’t exist yet. People who say Charlemagne was the Holy Roman Emperor are wrong.) His life is narrated by the Leading Player, a manifestation of his own desire to always be more. At the end of the show, the cast urges Pippin towards a great final act: his own death, the culmination of his self-destructive and depressive cycle. Having found love and family, Pippin finally realizes that by accepting an ordinary life, he is living an extraordinary one, having overcome the desire for more. Then, Theo, a character who is effectively his adopted son, sings Pippin’s big song, “Corner of the Sky,” indicating that the desire for more and the endless search for satisfaction have been passed on.

I spent so long summarizing Pippin because I think its story is absolutely brilliant. It’s among the best representations of the depths of a depressed mind I’ve ever seen. The players, who are effectively voices in his head, guide him towards a lack of satisfaction and eventually suicidal ideation. It’s very easy to miss this deeper facet entirely and take Pippin as a simple story about finding a deeper meaning to life. That was my take when I first watched it, although that was in 2018 when I was 13, so maybe I was just a little oblivious. What makes Pippin not just deep but transcendentally brilliant is the combination of these themes with what I think is easily Schwartz’s best score (that’s right, Wicked is heavily overrated, and we all know it).

I would recommend [this page](https://stephenschwartz.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/Pippinthemes.pdf) from Stephen Schwartz’s website that archives questions and answers from Schwartz on the meaning of Pippin.

# #6: West Side Story (1957)
**Music by Leonard Bernstein, Lyrics by Stephen Sondheim, Book by Arthur Laurents, Orchestrated by Sid Ramin and Irwin Kostal.**
![West Side Story](/rants/top10musicals/westsidestory.jpeg)

Remember when I said you’d see the name Stephen Sondheim again? Here he is. Writing the lyrics for West Side Story was his first Broadway credit. Don’t worry, you’ll see his name again.

West Side Story is what my father and I have agreed is “The Great American Musical.” It’s a retelling of Romeo and Juliet set in the Upper West Side of New York City in the 1950s. Instead of two warring families, West Side Story chronicles the conflict between two gangs: the Puerto Rican Sharks and the white Jets. As the war rages on, Tony, a former Jet, falls in love with Maria, the sister of Bernardo, head of the Sharks. After Bernardo kills Tony’s best friend, Tony kills Bernardo, and then later Tony is killed by Chino, a Shark trying to court Maria. The Sharks and Jets form a procession to take Tony’s corpse away to his final resting place.

This show is thoroughly genius. I am not nearly musically intelligent enough to break down the mastery that Bernstein exhibits in “The Dance at the Gym”, but when people smarter than me explained it to me, I believed them. Songs like “Tonight”, “America”, and “A Boy Like That” have become rightfully iconic in the world of musical theater. I think maybe the most interesting song is, strangely enough, “Maria,” where Tony exalts his new love by repeatedly saying her name. Of the 125 words in “Maria,” 29 of them are Maria. That’s 23% of the total lyrics. Yes, I counted. This song works because it perfectly exemplifies the character of Tony being in love with someone he barely knows, and all he can think to say is her name, exploring how it feels to say it in different ways each time. With a strong Tony, this oft-mocked song is incredible.

So, West Side Story is a masterpiece that eclipses the vast majority of musicals in quality and was created by some of the legends of the art form. Why does that not get it into the top 5? Well, there are a few problems with it. First is that Tony and Maria’s love at first sight feels incredibly surface-level, with little to nothing supporting it. This isn’t necessarily done any better or worse than in Romeo and Juliet, but it’s just as strange there. Second, Act 2 is not only nowhere near as good as Act 1, but it is also maybe half the length of the first. West Side Story feels incredibly imbalanced. Finally, the Act 2 opener “I Feel Pretty” is maybe the most cringe-inducing song Sondheim ever wrote lyrics for. [He even thinks they were not great](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HHN_KfXK5iU), but for different reasons than I do.

# #5: Next to Normal (2009)*
**Music by Tom Kitt, Lyrics and Book by Brian Yorkey, Orchestrated by Michael Starobin and Tom Kitt.**
![Next to Normal](/rants/top10musicals/n2n.jpg)

I once again hear you saying, “Marty? You’re ranking Next to Normal higher than West Side Story? Are you nuts?” Yes, I am ranking it higher, and yes, I am nuts. Next to Normal won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama and West Side Story didn’t, so bite me. I find this show to be one of the most touching I’ve seen, and easily the saddest I’ve seen.

Next to Normal follows the Goodman family: Diana, Dan, and their daughter Natalie, as Diana suffers from bipolar disorder. She sees the ghost of her deceased son, Gabe, everywhere she goes. Gabe died as an infant, but she sees him as a teenager, legitimately believing him to be alive. Diana goes through many different treatments that all fail to “cure” her. After a failed suicide attempt, she tries electroconvulsive therapy, which causes her to temporarily lose 19 years of memories. In the end, Diana leaves Dan, saying that they will never truly cope while together. Finally, Dan acknowledges the vision of Gabe, revealing that he has been suppressing his grief over his son for close to two decades.

Musically, it’s really great with standout numbers like “I Miss the Mountains,” “I Am the One,” and “I’m Alive.” Where I think this show really excels is in how it hides information from the audience that entirely recontextualizes how you interpret it. If there’s any musical on this list that merits multiple viewings to truly understand, it’s this one. The reveal that Gabe is dead doesn’t come till halfway through Act 1 and is shocking to many, but an observant viewer could catch it earlier. However, the reveal that Dan has seen Gabe the entire time is almost imperceptible, but it entirely changes how a repeat viewer will think about Dan as a character. One example is that, on an initial viewing, the moment in “I Am the One” where Gabe asks Dan why he won’t look at him can easily be explained as Diana thinking Gabe would confront Dan. A subsequent viewing of the show results in the heartbreaking realization that Dan can see Gabe in this scene and is unable to permit himself to acknowledge his dead son.

I love Next to Normal, but it suffers from the common ailment of Act 1 being better than Act 2. The Act 2 curse strikes again! Outside of this, I have basically no issues with this musical other than that Brian D’Arcy James was busy playing Shrek in Shrek the Musical during the original Broadway run, so the Original Broadway Cast Album has J. Robert Spencer as Dan, and I much prefer D’Arcy James.

While I know I said I wouldn’t be judging these by specific production, I just want to say that I think Jack Wolfe’s portrayal of Gabe in the 2024 London production is absolutely perfect and heartbreaking. I think he’s one of the most talented people in theater right now, and I wish he were in everything.

> I am the one who held you \
> I am the one who cried \
> I am the one who watched while you died

— Gabe and Dan, I Am the One (Reprise)

# #4: Hadestown (2019)
**Music, Lyrics, and Book by Anaïs Mitchell, Orchestrated by Michael Chorney and Todd Sickafoose.**
![Hadestown](/rants/top10musicals/hadestown.jpg)

This is one of two places where I will admit any real bias. I am a financial investor in the 2019 Broadway, 2021 North American Touring, and 2024 London productions. While this may influence my opinion, I am thoroughly convinced that I’d love Hadestown just as much if I were just a fan.

Hadestown is the most recent musical on this list, being only 7 years old at the time of writing, but I think it’ll stand the test of time. I also think it is easily the best musical of the 2010s (spoiler: no more 2010s musicals on this list. Sorry, Hamilton). Hadestown retells the Greek myths of Orpheus and Eurydice and Hades and Persephone with a jazzy Great Depression twist. Before I say the next part, consider that this part was conceived in 2006. The underworld has been reinterpreted as a factory town run by Hades, a greedy industrialist, whose residents are tasked with building a wall to keep out the impoverished. Explaining the story would take way too much time for this one, so I will simply heap it with praise instead. 

The music of Hadestown combines elements of folk, jazz, blues, and traditional showtunes to create a beautifully varied score that keeps you coming back time and time again, which actually fits the ending of the show quite well. Songs like “Road to Hell,” “Way Down Hadestown,” “Hey, Little Songbird,” and the 3 “Epics” really exemplify the genre-bending appeal of Hadestown. Hadestown makes mythical Greek gods feel like real and complex people. They are nuanced characters dealing with relatable problems like loss, loneliness, and complicated love. Meanwhile, from the human side, we get Eurydice, who feels like a real-life pragmatist who has to come to grips with a terrible choice, and Orpheus, a dreamer who can’t find the balance between love and duty.

The most devastating part of Hadestown is the ending, when Orpheus is a literal step away from saving Eurydice from death and fails. Consistently, the audience gasps when it happens; it’s a truly heartbreaking moment. Then, the show’s narrator, Hermes, tells the audience that they’re going to “sing it again” as the show restarts, Orpheus meeting Eurydice again, the audience now knowing it’s doomed to fail. It says that we will try again, even against seemingly impossible odds, thinking that “it might turn out this time.” It embodies what I think makes for the perfect ending to a musical: bittersweetness. Hadestown concludes with a tragedy, but with a message of hope. The idea that we can and should try again, no matter what.

It reads like I have no issues with Hadestown, and that’s because I don’t. The only reason that it’s not ranked as the best musical of all time is that we have what I consider the heaviest of hitters coming up. Its placement at #4 is not a mark of imperfection, but of the strength of competition.

Of the shows on my top 10, Hadestown is the only one that’s currently running on Broadway. So, I cannot recommend it enough to you. (and put a penny in my pocket) Additionally, there was a major film release of the London production with the original Broadway stars, which you can rent or purchase online. It’s easily one of the best filmed musicals I’ve seen. It doesn’t matter how you do it, but you should watch Hadestown.

For further analysis of Hadestown, I thoroughly recommend Anaïs Mitchell’s book [“Working on a Song: The Lyrics of HADESTOWN”](https://a.co/d/05oUO3hT) and [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XGF76aNf_o8) by Schaffrillas Productions.

# #3: Sunday in the Park with George (1984)
**Music and Lyrics by Stephen Sondheim, Book by James Lapine, Orchestrated by Michael Starobin.**
![Sunday in the Park with George](/rants/top10musicals/sunday.jpg)

Remember when I said you’d see the name Stephen Sondheim again? WELL YOU GUESSED IT! IT’S SONDHEIM TIME AGAIN! This was Sondheim’s first collaboration with James Lapine, and I think it's his best. Don’t worry, you’ll see Sondheim’s name again.

My affection for this musical is rather obvious, as the homepage of this website quotes it. Still, this is the pick I feel I will have to defend the strongest, as I don’t think it falls within many people's idea of what a top 5, or even top 10, musical should be. Sunday is not particularly epic in scale and doesn’t have intricate dance scenes or anything like that. However, if someone were to ask me, a non-artist, to recommend a piece of theater that explains how to be an artist, I would recommend Sunday. Sunday is entirely dedicated to the questions of what it means to be an artist, the costs of complete dedication to one’s art, and what it means to be original. 

The plot focuses on two central artists with different personal or artistic failings. The first is a fictionalized version of Georges Seurat, the inventor of pointillism; the second is a fictional descendant of Seurat, also named George. We will call Georges Sr. Seurat and George Jr. George. Seurat is unable to effectively express his feelings for his lover, Dot. She needs him to be present, but he can’t even go with her to see the Folies, because he “has to finish the hat.” He can never prioritize his personal connections over his art. Whether or not that is wrong is up to interpretation, but Sunday makes it clear that he doesn’t know how to do anything else. So, Dot leaves him. In Act 2, we switch perspective to Seurat’s great-grandson, George, who struggles with originality. He has created 7 light machines in the same series. They were clearly revolutionary once, but now they are just more of the same. Then, a vision of Dot appears to him and gives him Seurat’s words, “White. A blank page or canvas. So many possibilities.” The musical ends right there.

The two Georges are opposites with the same problem. Seurat sacrifices his life for his art, while George sacrifices his art for success, and neither of them is happy. George is obsessed with what others think of his work and whether he is being original; meanwhile, when someone doesn’t understand Seurat’s work, he tells the painting, “he does not like you. He does not understand or appreciate you… But we will not let anyone deter us, will we?” Both Seurat and George fail to find a balance between creating something original and living a life that doesn’t isolate them. This structure is one of the core reasons why Sunday is so brilliant. It gives us two perspectives on the same problem and makes clear the toll that creating art takes on a person. Dot instructs George, saying, “Anything you do, let it come from you, then it will be new. Give them more to see.” Sondheim is telling us that art need not be entirely original, but it should reflect how you truly feel and who you are, and if it does that, then it is original.

Many people argue that Sunday’s Act 2 is worse than Act 1. Is that true? Yes, but not to the degree that it is for many other shows. So, the Act 2 curse strikes again, but only a little. What makes Sunday different is that the entire point is Act 2. We don’t see the possibility of balancing art and life, and the message just becomes “Making art is hard” if you don’t have Act 2 to close out the story. Much like Sondheim and Lapine’s other collaboration, Into the Woods, Sunday is all about the second act.

I think Sunday is the most introspective and possibly most important musical I’ve ever had the joy of watching. Does that make it #1 on the list? Nope. The next two are the most objectively perfect musicals in creation. I cannot recommend the filmed 1986 proshot of the original Broadway production of Sunday enough. It will give you more to see.

For further analysis on Sunday, I recommend James Lapine’s [“Putting It Together: How Stephen Sondheim and I Created 'Sunday in the Park with George.'”](https://a.co/d/0hf21F2J)

# #2: Sweeney Todd: The Demon Barber of Fleet Street (1979)*
**Music and Lyrics by Stephen Sondheim, Book by Hugh Wheeler, Orchestrated by Jonathan Tunick.**
![Sweeney Todd: The Demon Barber of Fleet Street](/rants/top10musicals/sweeney.jpg)

So…. How about that Sondheim fella? I heard he’s written a good musical or two… WELL, IT’S ALWAYS SONDHEIM TIME HERE! I WILL RANT ABOUT HIS WORKS UNTIL THE COWS COME HOME (that phrase has never made sense to me).

I must once again admit bias. I was an investor in the 2023 Broadway revival of Sweeney, but I loved it and listed it as my second favorite musical long before I had that opportunity.

To put it simply, Sweeney is Sondheim’s masterpiece. I just showered Sunday in the Park with George with so much praise that it’s difficult to imagine me liking another Sondheim show more, but Sweeney is just… perfect. It is a rare musical in that there is literally not a single note, rhythm, word, or moment that I would change. Sweeney tells the story of a barber, Benjamin Barker, who was banished from Victorian England to Australia for the crime of a corrupt and evil judge lusting after his wife. After 15 years, Barker returns to England under the pseudonym Sweeney Todd. He hopes to find his wife, Lucy, and his daughter, Johanna, alive and well. Instead, he finds Lucy dead, having killed herself with poison, and Johanna in the captivity of the judge, who plans to marry her. After nearly killing the judge, he decides that he will kill anyone because “they all deserve to die.” His new companion, Mrs. Lovett, turns the corpses into meat pies, which become astoundingly popular. In the end, Sweeney finally kills the judge, but finds that Mrs. Lovett had lied to him, and that Lucy was not only alive when Sweeney returned, but that he had killed her, thinking she was just a beggar. Out of rage, he kills Mrs. Lovett and is then killed by Toby, a boy that Mrs. Lovett had taken in (after Sweeney killed his previous boss).

So, that’s a dark story. Why should something so unrepentantly dark rank so high? Because it’s fantastic. Everything from the music to the orchestrations to the book to the story is perfect. Numbers like “The Worst Pies in London” and “By the Sea” are hilarious, while others like “Epiphany” and “Pretty Women” are menacing. Then, numbers like “A Little Priest” manage to be both terrifying and make your abs hurt from laughing. Sweeney transitions between these feelings effortlessly, with undertones of fear and drama in every funny moment. The music itself is Sondheim’s best and easily his most complicated in terms of motifs. If you listen closely to almost any of the songs in Sweeney, you can find the dies irae hiding somewhere, symbolizing death following the characters everywhere they go.

Sweeney Todd himself may be the best vision of what a man going mad in front of the audience looks like. He starts the show as a damaged man who is on a simple mission of revenge to kill those who wronged him, and the audience can sympathize because the actions of the judge are comically evil. However, he goes further and further. First, he kills a man who tries to blackmail him. Then, he considers killing Toby because he might get suspicious. By the end of Act 1, he’s resolved to kill anyone and everyone. Watching him go from an understandable antihero to a demon in human form is a fascinating conundrum for the audience, in a way that is reminiscent of Walter White in Breaking Bad. One is forced to wonder at what point we stop rooting for Sweeney, if we ever do. 

We also can’t ignore Mrs. Lovett, who is almost like a Lady Macbeth to Sweeney’s Macbeth. It goes beyond the fact that they turn murder into a group project. Both Lady M. and Mrs. Lovett are the more practical of the respective pairs, and they are the ones taking the ambitious impulses of their partners and turning them into more concrete plans to get what they want. The other main point of this comparison to me is that both women open the door to further cruelty, propelling their partners to become unrepentant and irredeemable monsters, vastly eclipsing the malice of Lovett and Lady M.

This combination of a complex understanding of characters, one of the best scores to a musical ever written, and a dark atmosphere unrivaled by any other dark musical results in what can only be described as a seminal masterpiece that ought to be studied by anyone interested in writing musicals.

I recommend the filmed version of the 1982 tour starring Angela Lansbury and George Hearn. It’s a marvelous watch and was my introduction to Sweeney.

For further analysis, I once again recommend [a Sideways video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a4K-9Ekzc2A). This guy must really like Sondheim too. And with that, you won’t hear the name Sondheim again in this rant. Take a deep breath; you’re free.

# #1: Les Misérables (1987)*
**Music by Claude-Michel Schönberg, Lyrics by Alain Boublil and Jean-Marc Natel, book by Claude-Michel Schönberg and Alain Boublil, English Translation by Herbert Kretzmer, Orchestrated by John Cameron (modern orchestrations by Christopher Jahnke).**
![Les Misérables](/rants/top10musicals/lesmis.jpg)

I think we all knew this was coming. [My first rant on this website](/rants/eponine) was about a singular, specific problem I have with portrayals of Éponine. I’ve seen Les Mis so many times that I can’t even accurately count it anymore, but it’s north of 10.

Every metric I could choose for what makes a great musical is fulfilled by Les Mis. The music? Phenomenal. The orchestrations? Exemplary in their original and modern forms. The story? Riveting. The structure? Perfectly symmetrical. Featuring songs like “One Day More,” “On My Own,” and “Bring Him Home,” Les Mis has the best score in musical history. That’s not an opinion; that’s a fact. I’ll fight anyone who disagrees. The story is… far too long and complex to explain here; I’m already at over 5000 words. You want a great opening number? Take a bunch of prisoners chanting that they’re doomed dramatically. You want a great Act 1 ender? May I present “One Day More,” otherwise known as the best Act 1 ender ever. You want a great ending? How about a reprise of “Do You Hear the People Sing,” one of the most stirring songs ever written?

The most interesting thing about Les Mis, structurally, is how the music repeats itself. It’s not innovative to have leitmotifs that repeat in a symmetrical structure, but the frequency with which Les Mis does it and how effectively it conveys certain themes is a feat unto itself. This is something that I won’t be able to explain well here, so I once again invite you to watch [a Sideways video](https://youtu.be/1ikqU6G6Xgs?si=yeCYnS3lICwt8m_h). This rant really is an ad for Sideways. This one is about how terrible the movie adaptation of Les Mis is, which is true, but it covers the structure rather nicely.

As for the themes, Les Mis is about what it means to be redeemed and whether human beings can overcome their circumstances and their pasts. Jean Valjean is an ex-con who broke his parole and then became the most upstanding citizen in all of France; he overcomes poverty, incarceration, and guilt to become everything he never thought he could be. Javert fails to overcome the mistakes of his past, and it drives him to suicide. Les Mis tells us that forgiveness can’t just be given; it must be received. We have to learn to forgive ourselves, and I think there are few messages more important than that.

Jean Valjean and Javert are, in my not-so-humble opinion, the greatest hero and villain duo in all of musical theater. Both are acting out of their own view of justice, and neither is objectively right or wrong in their pursuits. As mentioned above, how they differ is how they respond to forgiveness and mercy. Valjean goes through one of the most incredible moral journeys ever depicted, going from a criminal convinced he was wronged by the world, willing to commit crimes again and lash out against the system rigged against him. By the end, he’s a loving father who’d sooner die alone and in tears than risk putting his daughter in harm’s way, about as stark a contrast as you can manage. Javert similarly goes through a moral journey. He begins the show convinced that some people are good and some are evil, concluding that Valjean is evil. Interestingly enough, it’s not Valjean’s mercy for Javert that leads him to suicide, but seeing him carry a wounded Marius, his future son-in-law, to safety after Marius was shot, that leads him to conclude his worldview is wrong. Javert’s entire job is to protect the innocent; he sees Valjean doing that and can’t make it align with his worldview. The song “Javert’s Suicide” itself fixates on the forgiveness he receives from Valjean, but I think I could really make a strong case for the Marius thing being what finally breaks him.

The more minor characters also embody complexity and are some of my favorites. Marius has to decide between love and justice. Éponine loves Marius but still helps him find his love, Cosette. Cosette is there too. She’s kind of a plot device more than anything, but that’s fine. The Thénardiers are some of the best comedic relief in any musical. Two of the characters I could talk about all day are Enjolras and Grantaire: the idealist and the cynic. To invoke Hadestown, Enjolras “sees the world as it could be.” Grantaire sees the world as it is. However, the one thing he believes in is Enjolras. Enjolras laughs in the face of the question of whether he would risk his life for justice. Grantaire is the only one of the revolutionaries who really recognizes the danger of rebelling, but he fights anyway. Why? Because he believes in Enjolras. It’s a beautiful mini-story within the main plot, and I just want to give Grantaire a giant hug.

Is Les Mis perfect? No. It should be longer. It should be three acts and four hours, and I know how I’d do that, but that’s a rant for another time. How can I say a show that could be improved is better than Sweeney, a show I said was perfect? Because, to invoke mathematics, there are different sizes of infinities. The infinity of the perfect parts of Les Mis is larger than the infinity of perfection that is Sweeney. If you don’t like that explanation, the simple version is: it’s peak. A show that has this many iconic numbers, some of the best characters ever put on stage, and the best score in musical theater history deserves the undisputed #1 position. My objectively correct case stands. Thank you for coming to my TED Talk.

How should you watch Les Mis? Not the movie. It’s a testament to garbage and inflicting emotional damage on me. The 10th anniversary and 25th anniversary concerts are pretty good. I’d recommend the 10th over the 25th entirely because of Nick Jonas as Marius.
