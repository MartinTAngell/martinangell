Before we get into this, it feels appropriate to comment on the current World Series, as I’m writing this the morning after Game 2. What a travesty. I say this from the unbiased perspective of a fan of the Chicago Cubs who hates the Milwaukee Brewers. Watching them coast to a World Series trophy is a miserable experience and somehow makes me miss watching the Los Angeles Dodgers win last year, which I didn’t know was possible. At least that series was fun to watch. I’m not the kind of person who is going to peddle conspiracy theories about “the rich teams are letting the Brewers win it all to avoid a salary cap.” I’m also not going to discredit those theories, because I do find it suspicious just how easily a team that has made nine errors in the postseason so far is beating every other team. Of course, by saying this at all, I peddled those theories, so maybe I am one to do that. 

Now, onto what I’m actually here to talk about. A simple change that I think would make MLB significantly better, and why I don’t think it’ll happen. Here’s my dream: no more automatic intentional walks. This does not prohibit throwing four pitches way outside the strike zone to walk someone. I’m against the automatic intentional walks introduced in 2017, where the manager can signal from the dugout to send a batter to first base. First, I will detail two situations that I think a great many people found genuinely frustrating, which would be fixed by this, and one recent event that shows why this would be such an effective fix.

Recall the 2025 World Series, which I briefly mentioned earlier. In Game 3, the Los Angeles Dodgers and Toronto Blue Jays clashed for 18 innings over 6 hours and 39 minutes. It tied the record for the longest World Series game in history, measured by innings. By the end of the seventh inning, Dodgers superstar Shohei Ohtani was 4-for-4 with two doubles and two home runs, the second of which tied the game at 5-5. Ohtani was intentionally walked in the 9th, 11th, 13th, and 15th innings. He was then semi-intentionally walked in the 17th inning. He finished his night having reached base 9 times with 4 hits and 5 walks. An insane stat line that we won’t see the likes of for a long time. However, that stat line was only possible because they went 18 innings, which might not have happened if they’d had to pitch to Ohtani, especially considering that when Ohtani came up to bat in the 13th, there was a runner on third. A bloop hit would’ve ended the game. I could’ve gotten so much more sleep if they’d just pitched to Ohtani. But you could argue that they would intentionally walk him with actual pitches if the automatic walk were banned. However, they’d have to ensure none of those pitches went wild or suddenly the runner on third runs like a bat outta hell and suddenly the Dodgers win. The automatic intentional walk incurs absolutely no risk to the defending side, while actually throwing outside pitches involves some. It also provides no advantage to the offense because they only need the runner on third to score to win, not the one on first. So, in this situation, it provides a clear advantage to the defense in that they get to skip a dangerous bat at no risk, and the offense can do nothing about it. 
![Shohei Ohtani's intentional walk in the 13th inning of Game 3 of the 2025 World Series](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kipXGXpfZ2E&start=26m53&end=27m18)

While I have no love in my heart for the Dodgers, I feel for their fans who had to pay at least $700 for admission only to spend an extra 3 hours watching their star get effectively sidelined. It leads me to another argument, which is that it’s not a fun experience for the fans. If we, as a collective baseball community, are going to call Ohtani the best in the game, which I don’t necessarily agree with in the age of Pete Crow-Armstrong, don’t we want to see the best player in the sport hit? When I do watch the Dodgers play, I’m not watching for Tommy Edman or Miguel Rojas; I’m watching for Shohei Ohtani (and Kiké Hernández). When even I, a hardcore Dodgers hater, get pissed watching Ohtani get waved to first for the 80th time in 20 minutes, it’s time to reconsider the rule. One thing I’m not considering here is Barry Bonds’ 688 intentional walks. He was doping (I think I legally have to say allegedly, so I will clarify: he was allegedly doping); he deserves no consideration. As a sidebar, I would recommend watching [Jeff Pearlman’s incredible video detailing how terrible a person Barry Bonds was while he was playing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M8z7Tg5Va9Y).

Now, onto the second situation that I think wouldn’t have been quite so miserable if you couldn’t just send a batter to first. I will now play my ancestral role as a Cubs fan hating the St. Louis Cardinals. In a Cubs vs. Cardinals game on September 27, 2025, the Cubs first baseman, Michael Busch, had hit two home runs, a triple, and a double. He was a single shy of the cycle, an accomplishment that has now been achieved only 353 times over the last 144 years of baseball. When Busch came up in the bottom of the 8th with the Cubs up 7-3, I was watching with unsurpassable excitement. I’d never seen a cycle before, and I was thoroughly convinced that I was about to see it. Then the Cardinals’ manager, Oliver Marmol, waved Busch to first base. I was incensed. I was booing at the screen and ranting about this exact thing in the family group chat. Afterward, Marmol said, “I’m not here for anybody’s amusement.” Yes, you are. Baseball is a sport, which is entertainment. You are here explicitly for entertainment, and you are on my screen to entertain me; I am not entertained. I would’ve loved to see Busch get a chance to make some history; I’m just thankful I got to see Pete Crow-Armstrong hit one this season. One could argue that they’d still intentionally walk Busch if we got rid of automatic intentional walks, but I’m about to show why that’s not necessarily true.
![Michael Busch is intentionally walked a single away from the cycle](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/_INXbJbO_wA)

So, why would forcing a pitcher to throw 4 pitches well outside of the strike zone be better for baseball than just waving someone over to first? Because throwing a pitch so far away that it's basically in Europe is no guarantee that it won't get punished. In Game 5 of the 2026 ALDS, José Ramírez of the Cleveland Guardians hit a home run against the Chicago White Sox. So, what’s special about it? The location of the pitch itself. It was 0.74 feet above the ground. Ramírez’s strike zone starts roughly 1.6 feet above the ground. No home run had ever been hit before on a pitch this low in a postseason game (since pitch tracking began in 2008), and it’s among the lowest ever hit.
![José Ramírez hits a pitch 0.74 feet above the ground for a homer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g-rp_U0hQ-I)

Now, a sharp reader will point out that the pitch in question was pretty much directly over the plate, and an intentional walk pitch will generally be wide, not low. However, that’s just part of the risk of having to pitch to someone you don’t want to. One mistake and your wide walk pitch gets just close enough that a hitter can do some damage. And beyond that, if it’s too wide, your catcher misses it and a runner on 2nd advances to third. If you can walk someone with a wave of your hand, there’s no risk of a bad pitch or a wild pitch. Why should a team be allowed to eliminate this risk? In the example I gave with Ohtani, if they’d had to pitch to him in the 13th, that runner on 3rd may have scored on a wild pitch. Who knows.

I mentioned that I don’t think this change will happen; why is that? Pace of play. The automatic intentional walk was an early change made to reduce the duration of MLB games, an initiative that has significantly ramped up since. I really don’t think that under the administration of Rob Manfred, MLB would give up any ground on any pace of play changes. Manfred prides himself on how his changes have made baseball more accessible by decreasing the length of the average game, and I’ll admit to being a huge fan of the pitch clock and the limit on disengagements. Still, I really don’t think the automatic intentional walk rule is consequential in the grand scheme of things. Intentional walks really don’t happen all that often if your name isn’t Barry “my head grew three sizes” Bonds, so the time saved over the course of a season is minimal. 

So, Rob, give us our four pitches back. As I said earlier, the job is to entertain us. I’m not entertained by watching a manager unceremoniously send the biggest stars in the game to first base in a blatantly unbalanced move that feels more like Banana Ball than Major League Baseball.

Don't look at me like that, Rob.
