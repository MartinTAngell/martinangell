**WARNING: Visual and storytelling spoilers for Ocarina of Time, Breath of the Wild, and Tears of the Kingdom.**

Among gamers whose opinions I respect, today is the most anticipated day in the month of November. You heard me, GTA VI. Go get delayed again! The Switch 2 remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time came out at midnight, and guess what? I can’t play it yet. I have to wait around at NYU for the next few hours, so I may as well write about my love for the Zelda franchise and what today means to me.

![The Trailer for The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (2026)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wuFfiTEr2yc)

I first played a Legend of Zelda game in 2013; I would have been 8. I discovered the franchise through the Zelda minigame in the Wii U release title, Nintendo Land. I loved that minigame and was desperate to play every Zelda game I could get my hands on; for my 8th birthday, I asked for all the available Zelda games on the Wii, Wii U, and 3DS. I quickly found out that Zelda games were nothing like that minigame. They were better.

The first Zelda game that I remember completing was Ocarina of Time 3D (2011) on the Nintendo 3DS. I was transfixed before the gameplay even began. Like many others, the sight of the title screen, with the gorgeous music associated with it, transported me into a world I never knew I needed. I believe I was on vacation at the time and couldn’t put down my 3DS, much to the chagrin of my parents. I recall a night on that trip with a torrential downpour and thus no reason to tell me to put my game away. That’s the night I beat Ocarina of Time. I vividly remember Zelda’s regret that Link never got to have a childhood. When Zelda sent Link back in time to live through his childhood, I cried. At the time, I didn’t quite know why, but I think I do now. Few other games have made me cry at the very end, maybe just Red Dead Redemption 2 and The Last of Us Part 2 (I have a rant about these two in my head that may come out at some point).

![The title screen animation from The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (1998)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F9ohCKS_LUM)

I think that the history of Ocarina of Time bears mentioning, as it’s a titan among video games. Ocarina of Time pioneered the 3D action-adventure video game in many technical and visual aspects. The most obvious contributions are Z-targeting and context-sensitive controls. However, I’d argue its most influential contribution was being one of the first games to really use the 3D world for strong visual storytelling. After the 7-year-long gap in the story, you don’t need someone to tell you that Ganondorf’s reign has sent the kingdom into ruin; you can see the before and after. And I’ll always appreciate Ocarina of Time for not shying away from being scary; the ReDeads and Dead Hands come to mind.

After I finished Ocarina of Time, I couldn’t put down my consoles. I played The Wind Waker, Twilight Princess, Skyward Sword, A Link Between Worlds, and even Hyrule Warriors. I have a funny anecdote about Hyrule Warriors if you’ll permit me a quick tangent. Right before the release of Hyrule Warriors, I did some stupid thing that I shouldn’t have done, although this was 12 years ago, so I forget what it was. Nevertheless, I was grounded. No video games for a week. For some reason, my dad and I had to go to the mall, and I asked to go into GameStop, where there was a demo of Hyrule Warriors set up. I think Dad and I had both forgotten I was grounded, so I played the demo. When we got home and Mom asked what we did at the mall, I told her, and I got my grounding extended by a few days for breaking the no video games rule.

In 2017, everything changed for the Zelda franchise with the release of The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild. The Zelda formula of introductory dungeons leading into an inciting incident and then a bunch more dungeons full of puzzles and bosses was thrown out in favor of open-world freedom. I got Breath of the Wild for my 12th birthday, but because we were not going to be home on my actual birthday, I was given it a day early. I stayed up until midnight waiting for it to download on my Wii U (which is not a bad console; I’ll die on this hill). Staying up till midnight is the norm for me now, but at 12 years old, it couldn’t have been stranger. Still, I refused to go to bed until I’d played this game. I had avoided all spoilers for the game until then. I’m so glad I did, because the sequence where Link climbs out of the Shrine of Resurrection and looks at the vast landscape that he can traverse made me feel the same way I did 4 years prior when I saw the title screen for Ocarina of Time.

![The Opening Cinematic of The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G8DNpB_k4CM&start=50)

Naturally, I could not have been more hyped for Breath of the Wild’s sequel, The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom (2023). The start was strong, and it had maybe my favorite depiction of Ganondorf ever. There are few moments in any game quite as jaw-dropping as the one I have included below. It’s too awesome for me to describe.

![The best moment from The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wVjdjoRzG2E)

However, Tears of the Kingdom, as a whole, fell a little short for me. I didn’t even fully come back to it and complete it until last summer. Why was that? Well, it felt like just Breath of the Wild, but more. I didn’t find myself falling in love with the new mechanics. They were very cool, but the novelty of blasting Koroks into space wore off quickly, and the best part of Tears of the Kingdom was the same as the best part of Breath of the Wild: exploring. But the world felt shallower than Breath of the Wild. The depths weren’t that interesting, and the sky islands got old fast. The biggest disappointment with both games was the dungeons. There were so few in both games, and the puzzles were obvious and somewhat uniform. When I finished Tears of the Kingdom, my first thought was, “I’d really love to play a classic Zelda game right now.”

Don’t get me wrong, I LOVE the Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom format. Exploring in those games feels entirely unlike any other game I’ve played, but we’ve been there, and we’ve done that. The Legend of Zelda built its status as one of the most beloved game franchises of all time on clever designs and puzzles that really made you step back and think. I’m sure some of the puzzles were made just so people had to buy the guidebook with the solutions. This is where I’d normally include a joke about the Water Temple, but honestly, it’s so over-hated that I won’t bother. I will acknowledge, though, that the 3DS version of Ocarina of Time made the Water Temple easier, so I didn’t suffer as my forefathers did. I’m curious if they’ve changed it more for the remake. My yearning for classic Zelda meant that when the trailer for the Ocarina of Time remake was released in September, I pre-ordered a physical copy within 5 minutes, which, as of my last check, should arrive at my apartment within the next two hours.

So, what am I hoping for from the remake? Honestly, I don’t know. I don’t know if I’ll feel good or bad about the changes they’ve made. I don’t know if the art style will be as appealing to me in gameplay as it looked in trailers. I don’t know if it can capture the magic of being an 8-year-old playing tennis with Ganondorf during a rainstorm. I don’t know if I’m in a place where this will hit me as hard as, or harder than, it did when I was young. What I do know is that there are very few games I’ve been more excited for in my life. Ever since getting to college, my mental bandwidth and allocated time for video games have plummeted. Being an adult has its sucky moments, I guess. So, it’s really refreshing for me to have that feeling of being a kid, waiting not-so-patiently to get home from school, boot up a console, and spend hours in Hyrule. The only change I really want is for it to be easier to skip past the goddamn owl’s rants. Somehow he rants more than I do.

In closing, I’ll tell you why I think I cried when I first finished Ocarina of Time. Link was being sent back in time to have the childhood that he had to forsake, and I think I was subconsciously realizing that the game was telling me to treasure that time of my life. Link is 10 at the start of Ocarina of Time, not much older than I was when I first played it. Now, I’m 21, and I’m so thankful I had the experience of growing up playing these games, watching them adapt and change as I did. The best example of this is just how different the art style of the original 1998 Ocarina of Time and the 2011 remake are from the one I’ll be playing tonight. So, when I get home and finish my work for the day, here’s my plan: I’m going to turn the lights off in my room, sit at the edge of my bed in my pajamas, and play a game I haven’t touched in 13 years. I got the childhood that Link never did, but he got to go back and live through it. I’m hoping that tonight, like Link, I’ll travel back and be my younger self again, transfixed by a world I never knew I needed.
